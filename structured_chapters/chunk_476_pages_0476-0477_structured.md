## Sanskrit Grammar (§ 780)

(a) The singular may be used to denote a class; @[siṃhaḥ] @[mṛgapadarājaḥ] the lion is the king of beasts; @[brāhmaṇaḥ pūjyaḥ] &c.

(b) The dual sometimes denotes a male and a female of the same class; @[pitarau] parents; @[caṭakau] a male and a female of sparrow.

(1) Note:—Words like @[dvaya], @[mithuna], @[yuga], @[yugma], &c. meaning 'a pair,' &c. which are dual in sense but singular in form ought to be always used in the singular, except when several pairs are meant.

(2) Note:—Words like @[hastau], @[akṣī], @[pādau], &c. should always be used in the dual in Sanskrit.

(c) The plural, like the singular, may represent a class, @[brāhmaṇāḥ pūjyāḥ] (or @[brāhmaṇaḥ pūjyaḥ]) Brahmanas (i. e. the Brahman class) are adorable.

(1) The plural is not unfrequently used as a mark of respect or reverence. @[iti śrīśaṅkarācāryāḥ]; so (says) the venerable Shankaracharya; @[iti ācāryadādaḥ] this is the opinion of the revered preceptor, &c.

(2) In the first person, great personages and writers sometimes use the plural instead of the singular, @[bhavantyaḥ kimapi pṛcchāmaḥ] we (i. e. I) too ask you something; @[iti brūmaḥ] but we (i. e. I, the writer) hold this opinion; @[vayamapi ca nirāmīmahe] we rule over speech (i. e. language).

(3) Words like @[dārāḥ], @[gehāḥ], @[akṣatāḥ], @[śilāḥ], @[āpaḥ], @[prāṇāḥ], @[havyāḥ], &c. are always used in the plural, though some of them are singular in sense.

(4) Names of countries which are really the names of the people inhabiting them, must be used in the plural; @[sa videhān upāyayau] he went to Videha, &c.

But in the case of compounds ending in words denoting a country, such as @[deśa], @[viṣaya], &c. the sing. must be used; @[asti māgadheśe pāṭaliputraṃ nāma nagaram] there is a town called Pataliputra in the country of Magadha.

(5) The plural of proper nouns denotes a family or race as in English; @[janakānāṃ rāmāṇāṃ ca yaśasvinaṃ bhojamaṅgalam] Utta.

## § 781-784 SYNTAX

### SECTION I.

### CONCORD.

§ 781. Concord is the agreement of words in a sentence as regards gender, number, person or tense.

The concords deserving notice in Sanskrit are three:—(1) Concord of the verb with the Subject; (2) Concord of the Adjective with the Substantive, and (3) Concord of the Relative with its Antecedent.

### CONCORD OF THE VERB WITH THE SUBJECT.

§ 782. The verb must agree with its subject in number and person; @[āsīt rājā nalo nāma] there was a king, Nala by name; @[ahaṃ gacchāmi] I go; @[dvau brāhmaṇau gacchatah] two Brahmanas go, &c.

§ 783. (a) When two or more subjects differing in number are connected by 'and' the verb must be plural; @[tataḥ kuntī ca pāṇḍavaḥ ca bhīṣmaśca saha bandhubhiḥ dadhuḥ udakaṃ tasya rājñaḥ] &c. Mah. Bha. Sometimes the verb agrees with the nearest subject in number, @[saha satyavatī devī gāndhārī ca yaśasvinī rājadeviḥ paritā gāndhārī cāpi niryayau] Mah. Bha. @[ahaśca rājaśca ubhe ca sandhye ceti jānāti narasya daivam]

(b) But when they are connected by 'or' and are all sing. the verb will be singular, and when the subject differ in number the verb will agree with the one nearest to it; @[rāmaḥ vā govindaḥ vā gacchatu] 'Let Rama or Govinda go,' @[eṣa vā ime vā bālakāḥ phalaṃ gṛhṇantu] 'let him or these boys take the mango fruit.'
